Chapter 21: Homework: Make Your Data Sing

Open in VS Code

In Chapter 19 and Chapter 20 you watched the Making Data Sing framework work on baby names and an online store. Now it's your turn, with your own data:

TYPE the columns → PAIR the types → apply a MOVE → look for a SHAPE → SHARPEN the question

You'll build one Malloy notebook that follows the same steps as Chapter 19, on a Parquet file hosted on the internet. By the end you'll have a short, honest data story: the findings that sing, and the ones that turned out flat.

What you turn in: one .malloynb file, with every cell run so the results are saved in the notebook.

Step 0 · Choose your data and put it online

Pick a dataset that can sing

Re-read Before you start: picking a dataset that can sing in Chapter 19. Your dataset must have:

  • at least four of the five column types (Who, Kind, When, Where, How much)

  • a When column that covers several years, or many months

  • one row per event (a sale, a game, a trip, a call), not a table someone already summarized

  • at least 10,000 rows

  • at least one door that opens: Order, Pair or Nest

Places to look:

  • Free Datasets for Social Science by Todd Jones: 150+ free datasets organized by topic, from crime and sports to real estate and shopping. A great place to start.

  • Chapter 17 · Data Sources: 20 ready-to-use datasets, and more places to search

  • your city's or state's open-data portal, data.gov, Kaggle, or Hugging Face datasets

Please don't use the baby-names, e-commerce or ICE data from this book. You've already seen what they can do.

Put it online as Parquet

Your notebook must read a Parquet file from a URL, not a file on your laptop. You have two options:

  • Host it yourself (recommended). Download a CSV, convert it to Parquet with zstd compression, and upload it to a public Hugging Face dataset or a GitHub release. Chapter 17's worked example shows every step, with the Washington electric-vehicle data.

  • Use a Parquet file someone else already hosts. Many Hugging Face datasets have a Parquet version (look for Auto-converted to Parquet), and some agencies publish Parquet directly. Check that the link ends in .parquet and opens without a login.

🔓 Only public data. Upload only data that is already public and whose license allows sharing. Never upload private data, such as data from an employer or with personal information. At a company, the same file would go to a private bucket in Google Cloud Storage or Amazon S3.

What your notebook must contain

Use these section headings, in this order. Every Malloy cell must run. After each result, write one or two sentences in plain English saying what the result shows. A query without a sentence after it doesn't count.

Part 0 · About the data

A markdown cell with:

  • the dataset's name, who published it, and a link to the original source

  • your public Parquet URL, and where you host it (Hugging Face, GitHub, or someone else's site)

  • what one row means ("one row per 311 call")

  • the number of rows and the dates it covers

  • if you converted it yourself: the CSV size, the Parquet size, and the compression you used

Then a Malloy cell that defines your source on the URL, with the dimensions and measures you need. Add to it as you go.

Part 1 · TYPE

  • a Malloy cell showing 5–10 raw rows

  • a markdown table naming the job of every column you use: Who, Kind, When, Where or How much

  • one sentence on a column with two jobs, if you have one (for example, a state that also nests in a region)

Part 2 · PAIR

  • The flat answer: the first, obvious query (group by one column, sum, sort). Say why it's flat.

  • Four pairs: four queries, each pairing two different types, such as Who × When, Who × Where, Kind × When, or How much × anything. Label each one with its pair, as in Chapter 19.

Part 3 · MOVE

At least one query from each family, labeled with the move:

Family Choose at least one
⚖️ Rescale share (all()), rank (rank())
↔️ Contrast change over time (lag() or filtered measures), over-index, top-N within each group (nest:)
🔦 Spotlight peak / first / last, concentration (top 10 = what % of the total?), a reversal, or the identifier as data

A query can count for Part 2 and Part 3 at the same time, if you label both.

Part 4 · SHAPE

  • For each of the three doors (Order, Pair, Nest), write one sentence: does it open for your data, and why or why not?

  • Write a query for at least one door that opens. For example: row_number() or lag() within an entity (Order), a self-join or a pipeline on a shared key (Pair), or a nest: down a hierarchy (Nest).

Part 5 · SHARPEN

Take three seeds and grow each one into a full question. For each one:

  1. a five-part table: subject, measure, move / shape, comparison, and your expected answer, written before you run the query

  2. the full question as one sentence

  3. the query and its result

  4. two or three sentences: were you right? What surprised you?

Part 6 · Compared to what?

Chapter 20 showed two answers that fell apart under a fair comparison: growth in a partial year, and a "season" that was really the store growing. Do one fairness check on your own data, and show the query before and after:

  • a partial period (compare like with like)

  • a raw count that changes when you rescale it to a share of the total

  • a top-N result that changes when you filter out small groups (as with utah_births >= 500 in Chapter 19)

If your answer survived the check, say so. That's a result too.

Part 7 · The data story

A short markdown section with:

  • your three best findings, each written as one "singing" sentence: specific, with a number and a comparison. Not "Tesla is popular," but "Tesla's share of new EVs in Washington fell from 64% in 2020 to 36% in 2025, even as total EV sales more than quadrupled."

  • one result that came back flat, and what that tells you

  • one limit of your data: something it cannot tell you

  • one question you'd ask next

An example to follow

Here's what the start of a good notebook looks like, using the Washington electric-vehicle data from Chapter 17. (You can't use this dataset for your homework.)

Part 0. Electric Vehicle Title and Registration Activity, Washington State Department of Licensing (data.wa.gov). One row = one title or registration transaction for an electric vehicle. 1,926,507 rows, 2010 to September 2026. Converted from a 934 MB CSV to a 30 MB Parquet file (zstd) and hosted on Hugging Face.

source: wa_ev is duckdb.table('https://huggingface.co/datasets/24601p/public-data/resolve/main/wa_ev_registrations.parquet') extend {
  dimension:
    // The dates are text like "December 20 2024"
    transaction_dt is try_strptime!timestamp(transaction_date, '%B %d %Y')::date
    is_new_sale is transaction_type = 'Original Title' and new_or_used_vehicle = 'New'
  measure:
    transactions is count()
    new_ev_sales is count() { where: is_new_sale }
}

  aggregate:
    new_ev_sales
    # percent
    tesla_share is new_ev_sales { where: make = 'TESLA' } / new_ev_sales
  order_by: sale_year

SELECT 
   DATE_TRUNC('year', (CAST((try_strptime(base."transaction_date",'%B %d %Y')) AS DATE))) as "sale_year",
   COUNT(CASE WHEN ((base."transaction_type"='Original Title') and (base."new_or_used_vehicle"='New')) THEN 1 END) as "new_ev_sales",
   ((COUNT(CASE WHEN (base."make"='TESLA')
  AND (((base."transaction_type"='Original Title') and (base."new_or_used_vehicle"='New'))) THEN 1 END)))*1.0/(COUNT(CASE WHEN ((base."transaction_type"='Original Title') and (base."new_or_used_vehicle"='New')) THEN 1 END)) as "tesla_share"
FROM 'https://huggingface.co/datasets/24601p/public-data/resolve/main/wa_ev_registrations.parquet' as base
WHERE (((base."transaction_type"='Original Title') and (base."new_or_used_vehicle"='New')) and (DATE_TRUNC('year', (CAST((try_strptime(base."transaction_date",'%B %d %Y')) AS DATE)))>=DATE '2015-01-01')) and (DATE_TRUNC('year', (CAST((try_strptime(base."transaction_date",'%B %d %Y')) AS DATE)))<DATE '2026-01-01')
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1 ASC NULLS LAST

Tesla's share of new EV sales in Washington peaked at 64% in 2020 and fell to 36% in 2025, even though total new EV sales grew from about 12,000 to 53,000 a year. Tesla didn't shrink; everyone else caught up. (2026 is left out because the data ends in September.)

Notice the parts of a good entry: the pair and move are labeled, the query answers one clear question, and the sentence after it gives specific numbers and a comparison, plus a note about a partial year.

Tips

  • Start small. Get your source working on the URL and run select: * limit: 10 before anything else.

  • Write your guesses first in Part 5. If you write them after, you'll always have been right, and you'll learn nothing.

  • Filter out tiny groups before you rank by a ratio or a share. A category with 3 rows can have a 100% rate.

  • Check partial periods. If your data ends partway through a year, compare like with like.

  • Flat is fine. If a question comes back flat, say so. Don't invent a story from small differences.

  • Stuck on syntax? Each step has a matching query in Chapter 19 or 19. Copy it and change the names.

  • Errors? See Chapter 18 · Troubleshooting.

Turning it in

Upload lastname_make_it_sing.malloynb. Before you save:

  1. Run every cell, top to bottom, so all results are saved in the file.

  2. Open your Parquet URL in a private or incognito window to check that it's public. Leave it up until grades are posted.
